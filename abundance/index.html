Abundance

2026-11-27 · friday

Disclaimer: I wrote this with Claude Code running Claude Opus 5.5, which built the interactive page, the figures and the notes, and drafted some of the connecting passages from my notes and session logs, which I then edited.

I find my conversations with AI almost always are an evolution of intent. I start with thinking I know about where I want to go, only to find it evolves the more context gets added.

For a long time I treated that as the agents getting things wrong. When I finally went back through my own sessions, I found that more than half of the times I had stopped an agent were about what I wanted, and most of those were me changing my mind after seeing its work. Now that almost anything I can think of already exists in some form, I think search and the agents we build with should help me find out what I need sooner, instead of searching harder for what I first asked.

The clearest case was a side project I worked on over the last few months. It started off as a custom harness with a formal proof system, then a platform for orchestration of agents, and then I realized I didn’t know what I didn’t know and pivoted entirely. By a formal proof system I mean that the harness only accepted an agent’s change once the change cited its evidence and passed the checks I had declared up front.

A few of my prompts from those sessions:

side project · prompts
  1. oct 4

    The harness is not able to educate me when it should which leads to wasted cycles. I am not to be assumed as the all knowing intellect but rather the most invested party in the outcome.

  2. oct 21

    No need to keep the tracked research corpus, it's noise by now.

  3. oct 28

    …that again is me doing the same dance within this session. That principle is what I often call the "I don't know what I don't know" and the idea that the harness should be able to sniff/understand that out of me and be able to course correct and steer before it has already wasted many turns or produced a lot of experiment and sometimes noise.

Some of that drift came from what I learned while building, and some from what kept launching around me. Between early October and early November I brought the agent a new tool, launch or post to study almost every week, and on October 22 I asked it whether what I was building already existed in some form. Its agents ran 74 web searches in four minutes and came back saying that no single product did all of it, but that every piece of it existed somewhere. A week later it pointed out that this was the fourth time a layer I had planned to build had turned out to be someone else’s free infrastructure. That flood of new tools didn’t start with AI, though.

The attention economy capitalized on the value of an always-online network of fragmented attention. It boosted the advertising industry, gave birth to influencers, and changed how consumers engage with brands.

Later influencers capitalized further on it to create their own brand and market value, with things like digital products and custom content, leading to a new creator economy.

Recently I saw an interesting graph, it shows how many new products are being launched on Product Hunt in the age of AI.NOTE 1More launches, not more winners. Kim, Kang & Song studied 160,000+ Product Hunt launches. Entry rose sharply after ChatGPT’s release, driven disproportionately by solo founders, and much of it was low-commitment and experimental. Team ventures still hold the top rankings. In the counts below, launches rose 41% in 2023, held about flat in 2024 and nearly doubled in 2025. arXiv:2605.10291, 2026. It’s as if everyone now wants to both be an influencer and a creator.

Now I find we are living in an era of abundance, almost everywhere you look it feels like there is too much of anything and everything.

That changes what search has to do. When thousands of results match a query, finding every one of them matters less than getting the first few right, and which few are right depends on who is asking and why. How do you return what the user wants when far more content matches their query than they will ever read, and when an agent searching on their behalf can only keep so much of it in view at once?NOTE 2Retrieve wide, keep few. Uber Eats search pulls in tens of thousands of candidates, and ranking discards most of them: “retrieval gathers a large set of candidates, and ranking later determines which results are worth keeping.” When the team halved search latency, part of the win was cutting broad keyword retrieval that added “little incremental value.” Sheth et al., Halving the Time, Uber Blog, 2026. What is noise and what is value? Even worse, how do you anticipate when they are wrong and should offer advice or corrections?

Looking back, the harness should have anticipated that I did not know what I did not know. A question helps with reflection and also with exposing signal to noise ratio. The system could have also picked up on context outside of its own space and that it also does not know what it does not know, that it operates from assumptions just like I do.

On October 28 I had the agent go back through 752 of my Claude Code sessions since February and classify the 475 times I had stopped or overruled an agent. More than half of them were about intent: in 35% I changed what I wanted after seeing the work, and in another 19% the agent had misread or overstepped what I wanted, while an agent lacking a tool or some knowledge explained fewer than one in ten.

When the agent then tried to predict, from each request alone, the specific thing I would raise, it got it right 6% of the time, although it named the right area about half the time. Before each of those changes of mind an agent had already spent a median of 12 minutes and 12 tool calls, such as searches, file reads and commands, on work I was about to redirect. I had been finding out what I wanted by reacting to what the agents made, and counting most of those reactions as their failures.

Granted, that’s sort of a skill issue on my part. It can be tempting to forget this so-called superintelligence that we have is nothing more than an illusion, especially because through text much of the nuance is lost. It can’t tell my body language, vitals, and any other signal that could help inform if I am using it from a place of true enlightenment or total brainrot slop dopamine-chasing vicious cycle (anyways… we will save the slot machine gambling of AI for another post, maybe).

I think search and generation are moving towards a progressive disclosure of complexity.NOTE 3Progressive disclosure. An interface pattern: show the few things most people need first, and reveal the rest on request. The Xerox Star team used it in the early 1980s; Jakob Nielsen’s 2006 article made the term common. It means meeting the user where they are, not where the data is.NOTE 4Search answered by the recommender. Spotify sends each search through a small language model that guesses the intent first, then routes it, sometimes to several tools at once. “New indie rock releases” can become a request to the personalized recommender; “latest albums by Lady Gaga” also covers a recent single the listener may have loosely called an album. The router sees only the query; personalization happens in the tools it calls. Palumbo et al., You Say Search, I Say Recs, Spotify Research, 2025. Using what a system can learn about my current task and session, not only the words I typed, then fitting the results to what I am trying to do right now rather than to a profile of what I did before.

A system should start from the little I can say at first, put something concrete and cheap in front of me early, a sample, a sketch or a short list instead of a finished answer or twenty pages of results, and reveal more of the space as my reactions show what I care about. Questions help when they are about a decision that would change the outcome, but in my sessions the agent mostly asked about what it didn’t know rather than about what I would care about, and 29 times I skipped its multiple-choice options to type my own answer. If I had to measure it, I would count how long it takes before I react to something concrete, and how many turns pass before my intent stops changing.

On October 22 I asked the agent to make a short video showing how the whole platform would work end to end, and watching it made me ask whether it already existed. When the agent looked back at that week on October 28, it pointed out that my reactions to concrete things, the video, the map of competitors and the data from my own sessions, had moved us forward more than any of its questions.

Because of all this abundance, I think we are entering the era of discovery tools. With almost anything I can think of already built, or a few prompts away from being built, the problem has moved from finding options to choosing between them, the paradox of choice. The tools I am looking forward to will meet me where I am, put a handful of good fits in front of me early, and let my reactions do the rest.
